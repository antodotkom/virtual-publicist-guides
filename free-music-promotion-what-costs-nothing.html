Free Music Promotion: What Actually Costs Nothing (and What Only Looks Free)

"Free music promotion" is one of the phrases independent artists search most, and one of the most misleading. Some routes really are free. Others are free to start and cost money later, and a few are "free" because you pay with your reputation or your catalogue. This guide sorts them into three groups and ends with a simple week-long plan that costs nothing but time.

Updated November 2026: refreshed against the current Spotify for Artists and KEXP pages listed under Sources.

Genuinely free

Spotify editorial pitching. Pitching an unreleased song to Spotify's editors through Spotify for Artists costs nothing. Spotify says a song pitched at least seven days before release is added to your followers' Release Radar playlists, so an early pitch is worth sending even though editorial placement is never guaranteed. That means delivering the song to your distributor well ahead of release day.

Radio submissions. Many college, community and public stations accept music from independent artists at no cost and publish how they want it sent. KEXP's "Getting Airplay" guide, for example, says non-commercial stations, many of them licensed to universities, are "your best bet for airplay" along with low-power FM and streaming stations, and suggests sending both a stream link and a download link.

Personal pitches to blogs and curators. Emailing a writer or curator who covers your genre is free. The cost is the time it takes to research each person and write a message that shows you know their work.

Your own channels. Your email list, social accounts, website and live shows are free to use, and they belong to you. They are also where every other kind of promotion sends people.

Collaborations. A feature, a remix swap or a shared show costs effort and coordination rather than cash, and each artist brings their own audience.

Free to start, paid later

Submission platforms with paid upgrades. Many let you send a few submissions free, then charge for credits, priority or more reach. That can be a fair deal if you understand it, but it is a paid service with a free trial, not free promotion.

Playlist offers with strings attached. Some curators ask for follows, shares or a fee after the fact in return for a placement. Read the terms before you agree to anything.

"Free" that costs the most

Bot-driven streams. Spotify's artificial streaming page says services that promise streams or playlist placements in exchange for money are scams, that many of them use bots to inflate stream counts, and that Spotify strictly prohibits using them. The risk to your music and your account makes this the most expensive "free" of all.

Questions artists ask

Can free promotion really work? Yes, if you are patient and focused. It works best when you pitch fewer, better-matched people and keep releasing music.

What should I do first? Fix your profiles and assets, deliver early, pitch Spotify's editors, then reach out to blogs, curators and stations that fit.

When is spending money sensible? On things that improve the music and how it is presented, such as mixing, mastering, photos and artwork. Be wary of anything that promises guaranteed results.

How do I spot a bad offer? Watch for guarantees, pressure to pay quickly, vague numbers, and playlists with large follower counts but little sign of real listeners.

A free promotion week, step by step

  • Monday: update your profiles and build a short list of about twenty contacts whose recent work fits your song.
  • Tuesday and Wednesday: send ten personal pitches each day.
  • Thursday: submit to two or three radio stations, following each station's published rules.
  • Friday: share the release with your fans and thank anyone who helped.
  • The following week: one polite follow-up per contact, then log the results.

Time is the real cost

Free promotion trades money for hours. Researching one well-matched blog or curator and writing a personal pitch can easily take twenty minutes or more, so a list of twenty contacts is a full day's work for one release. That is normal, and it is why artists who do well with free routes plan ahead and work in short daily blocks rather than one exhausting weekend.

The work compounds. People who replied once are easier to reach next time, and a simple log of every pitch, reply and placement becomes, over a year, a list of people who already know your music.

The short answer

Truly free promotion uses official routes, personal outreach and your own channels. It costs time instead of money. Anything that promises guaranteed results for free, or for a small fee, deserves a second look.
