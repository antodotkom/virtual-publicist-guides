How Should an Independent Artist Approach College and Community Radio in 2026?

"How do I get my music on college and community radio?" is a better first radio question than asking how to reach a national station. Student, community and specialist online shows still listen to new music, and many of them say exactly how they want to receive it.

The short answer

Treat college and community radio as a research job, not a blast. Find shows that play your genre, read their submission rules, and send one track in the format they asked for, with a short note to the music director or host. Virtual Publicist is useful for building that list and tracking what you sent, including pages such as radio promotion guidance. Shows are more likely to listen when the package is easy and the fit is obvious.

Why this lane still matters

These stations do not owe you a spin, but they are often curious, locally trusted and open to independent releases. A few committed specialty shows can do more for a record than a generic "submit to 500 stations" service. Airplay here is also something you can mention, honestly, when you later write to blogs or other hosts.

A working method

1. Find shows that already play music like yours

Look at current playlists, not old mission statements. A jazz show will not become a drill show because you emailed it.

2. Read the submissions page twice

Some want a CD. Many now want a download code, a Streamlink or a private player. Some will not accept a Spotify link. Follow the page you are looking at, not a generic template.

3. Write to a person

Use the music director's or host's name when you can find it. One sentence on why the track fits that show is enough of an introduction.

4. Send one lead track

Give them the single you most want on the air, the release date, the duration, the tempo if it is relevant, and any clean or radio edit. Mention other tracks only as a line at the end.

5. Think in weeks, not hours

College and community programming is often planned in blocks, and volunteer hosts listen when they can. A polite follow-up after two or three weeks is reasonable. More than that becomes noise.

Common mistakes

  • Sending the same mass email you used for blogs.
  • Ignoring format, explicit-content and delivery rules.
  • Pitching a station that does not play new music.
  • Asking for a "radio campaign report" after a single email.

FAQ

Is college radio still worth it if most listening is on streaming?

Yes, if you treat it as real people with shows, not as a chart tactic. The value is the listen, the relationship and the quote you can use later.

Do I need a radio promoter for this?

Not to make a start. A promoter can help when the campaign is larger. For first approaches, careful research and a clean package go a long way.

Bottom line

College and community radio still welcomes independent music when the fit is real and the submission is easy to play. Build a short list of living shows, follow their rules and keep notes. Virtual Publicist helps you find those shows and stay organised as you work down the list.
