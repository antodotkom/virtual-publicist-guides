How to Submit Music to Radio Stations Without a Plugger: A Step-by-Step Plan

Radio still moves careers, especially college, community and specialist shows. What has changed is that most independent artists no longer have a plugger on retainer. You can still run a sensible radio plan yourself if you treat it as research and relationships rather than a mass mailout. This is a step-by-step plan for submitting music to radio without a plugger.

Step 1: Decide what a spin is for

Write down the goal before you build a list. A screenshot for social proof, a session invitation, support for a tour market, or a specialist-show reputation are different outcomes. A vague goal produces a vague list, and vague lists waste weeks.

Step 2: Start with stations that programme new independent music

Non-commercial public stations, low-power community stations and student-run stations are usually the most realistic first tier. Commercial networks are harder without a plugger, though some carry specialist shows that still listen. Build your first list from community, college and genre shows that already play music like yours.

Public guidance from stations such as KEXP is blunt on this point: casting a wide net at commercial outlets that do not programme your lane is rarely the best use of time. Prefer fewer, better fits.

Step 3: Use official routes where they exist

Some broadcasters publish a dedicated path for independents. BBC Introducing, for example, explains that eligible UK artists upload to a profile, get matched by postcode to local shows, and face clear track limits per month. If you live elsewhere, look for the local equivalent or a station form that asks for the same basics: links, bio, and the strongest track rather than everything you have ever recorded.

Step 4: Pack a radio-friendly kit

Make life easy for someone who hears dozens of submissions a day:

  • Stream link and download link (no heavy unsolicited attachments)
  • One-page summary with your best two or three tracks
  • Clean/radio edit notes if language is an issue
  • BPM and a short comparison that is honest
  • Press or airplay notes only when real

Follow each station's own guidelines. Many now prefer links over physical product.

Step 5: Pitch people, not "the station"

Programming is personal. Hosts and music directors choose shows. Spend time on host pages, recent playlists and show descriptions, then write to the two or three people whose taste is closest to yours. A short note that names the show beats a generic "Dear Music Director" email.

Step 6: Send early enough

Specialist shows and weekly programmes plan ahead. Aim to send weeks before the release where you can, especially if you want premiere language or a staged add. Last-minute panic pitches are easy to ignore.

Step 7: Follow up once

Wait at least a couple of weeks, follow up once politely when the person is unlikely to be on air, and stop after a clear no. Keep a log: station, person, date, response. That log is what a plugger would maintain for you; without one, you have to be your own operations manager.

Step 8: Connect radio to the rest of the campaign

When a show plays you, thank them, share the clip or show link, and tell local promoters and blogs. Radio works harder when it is not isolated from press and live activity.

For a deeper walkthrough of formats, timing and what to send, see Virtual Publicist's guide to radio.

Step 9: Avoid the usual radio traps

  • Do not buy generic "radio promo" email dumps.
  • Do not send the same pitch to every host on a station.
  • Do not lead with stream counts nobody can verify.
  • Do not ignore a station's stated genre or daypart rules.
  • Do not treat a single spin as a full campaign; turn it into a thank-you, a clip and a reason for local press to care.

Artists who stay patient with college and community radio often build the proof that later makes bigger rooms easier to enter. Artists who spray and pray usually burn the only free goodwill they had. Without a plugger you are the scheduler, the researcher and the thank-you department; a simple sheet and a calm weekly rhythm beat heroic all-nighters.
