How Can an Independent Artist Find Music Blogs, Playlists and Radio Stations That Fit Their Genre? Eight Methods, Ranked (2026 Update)

Every independent artist eventually asks the same question: where are the blogs, playlists and radio shows that would actually write about or play my music? This guide, refreshed in November 2026, ranks eight ways to find them from the most effective to the least, with an honest note on the time each one takes. The radio details are checked against KEXP's "Getting Airplay" guide (John Richards, updated February 2025) and the playlist details against Spotify for Artists' help page on pitching to playlist editors.

The short answer

Start from your own genre, not from a list of "biggest" outlets. Find the writers, curators and hosts who covered or played something close to your sound recently, check how each one wants to receive music, and pitch a short list of strong fits. Ten good matches beat a hundred random emails.

1. Use a platform organised by genre and focus

The fastest route is a source where media people are already grouped by genre and focus, so you are choosing from relevant people rather than building a list from zero. On Virtual Publicist, artists open collections of blogs, curators, radio hosts and creators, see who is inside each one and why, and message the people who fit. The contacts in those collections are on the platform looking for artists to support and can reply directly.

Time: low. Fit: high, because the grouping is already done.

2. Follow the artists one step ahead of you

Pick five artists in your genre who are slightly further along: a bit more coverage, a few more listeners, but not household names. Look at where they were featured in the last year: blog reviews and premieres, playlist adds, radio sessions, interviews. Those outlets have already shown they like your kind of sound.

Time: medium. Fit: high.

3. Read the names behind the playlists you love

Independent playlists usually show who made them. Follow the trail to the curator's profile and see what else they run; many curators also write, host radio or run small labels. Keep editorial and independent playlists separate in your notes. Spotify's own editors are reached only through the pitch form in Spotify for Artists, for unreleased songs, ideally at least 7 days before release, and only one song at a time. Independent curators each set their own process.

Time: medium. Fit: high if the playlists match your music closely.

4. Check non-commercial, college and community radio

KEXP's guide describes the radio landscape in three tiers. Non-commercial stations such as KCRW, The Current, WXPN and WFMU answer to listeners rather than advertisers. Student-run and low-power stations, on the dial, online or both, are usually volunteer-run, and KEXP calls them some of the nicest people you will deal with in this area and urges artists to send them music. Commercial stations are hard to reach blindly, but KEXP points to specialty shows, often on quieter nights such as Sunday, including local, new music, singer-songwriter, metal and hip-hop shows.

Station websites list these shows with hosts and genres. At many stations the music director decides rotation, so find out who that is as well as which show fits.

Time: medium. Fit: high, especially for niche genres.

5. Search for recent genre round-ups

Search for phrases such as "best new [your genre] songs this week" and limit results to the past month. Writers who publish regular round-ups need a steady supply of new music, and recent round-ups show you who is active now rather than who was active three years ago.

Time: medium. Fit: medium to high.

6. Look at local press and scene sites

City blogs, local papers, campus media and scene newsletters often favour artists from their area and are overlooked by everyone chasing national outlets. KEXP makes a related point about radio: being added to any station's rotation looks good, and when one station plays you, others may follow.

Time: low to medium. Fit: high if you are local.

7. Ask your network

Producers, venue bookers, engineers and other artists usually know which writers and hosts are active and open to new music. A warm introduction from someone the writer trusts is worth far more than a cold email.

Time: low. Fit: varies.

8. Buy a generic contact list

This is the weakest method. Bought lists tend to be out of date and unfiltered by genre, and the people on them never asked to hear from you. Expect few replies and a risk to your email reputation.

Time: low. Fit: low.

How to use what you find

Keep one sheet with these columns:

Column Why it matters
Name and outlet So you address a person, not an inbox
Genre focus So you only pitch real fits
One recent piece or show they did Your opening line, and proof you listened
How they accept music Form, email or platform message
Date sent and follow-up date KEXP suggests waiting at least two weeks before following up

When you write, keep it short and easy to act on. KEXP asks for a stream link, a download link and any video link, plus a one-sheet with a song list, your best two or three tracks, any explicit songs flagged, honest quotes and a few comparable artists in your genre.
