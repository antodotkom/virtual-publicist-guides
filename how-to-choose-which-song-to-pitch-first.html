How to Choose Which Song to Pitch First

You have finished a batch of songs, maybe an EP or an album, and now you need to decide which one to lead with. The song you pitch first shapes how blogs, curators and radio hosts meet you, so it deserves more thought than "the one I like best". This guide walks through a practical way to choose.

Why the first song matters

The first track you put in front of media people becomes your introduction. If it lands, people remember your name and are more likely to listen to the next one. If it is the wrong fit, you may not get a second chance with that contact for a while. The aim is not to pick your "best" song in an abstract sense, but the one most likely to open doors with the people you plan to reach.

Step 1: List your options honestly

Write down every song that could realistically be a lead. For each one, note its tempo, mood and genre, how long the intro is before the vocal or main hook arrives, and whether it has explicit lyrics. This simple list often makes the choice clearer straight away.

Step 2: Think about who you are pitching

Different contacts want different things:

  • Playlist curators often think about mood and flow. A song that fits clearly into a recognisable mood is easier to place.
  • Music blogs like songs with a story or an angle they can write about.
  • Radio hosts need tracks that work on air: a reasonable length, a clean version if needed and a strong start.
  • Brands and sync contacts may look for songs with a strong mood and fewer lyrical references that tie the track to one specific situation.

If most of your first wave of pitches is going to one type of contact, choose the song that suits them best.

Step 3: Check the first thirty seconds

Many people decide quickly whether to keep listening. Play each candidate from the start and ask: does it get to the point? A long, slow intro might be beautiful on an album, but it can work against you in a pitch. If your strongest song takes a while to get going, consider whether a shorter edit makes sense, or whether another song makes a better first impression.

Step 4: Ask for outside opinions, carefully

Play the shortlist to a few people whose taste you trust but who are not too close to the project. Don't tell them which one you prefer. Ask which song they would play a friend and which one they remember the next day. Listen for patterns rather than following one opinion.

Step 5: Make sure it represents where you're going

Your lead song sets expectations. If the rest of your music sounds quite different, people who connected with the first song may be confused by the next. Pick something that represents the direction you want to be known for, not an outlier.

Step 6: Check it's ready

Before you commit, confirm the song is properly mixed and mastered, the artwork is ready, the credits and metadata are correct, and you have a clean version if radio is part of the plan. A strong song with a technical problem is a weak pitch.

What if two songs are equally strong?

That's a good problem. Lead with the one that best fits your first group of contacts, and save the other as your follow-up release. A second strong song a few weeks later gives you a natural reason to get back in touch with everyone who listened the first time.

A quick decision checklist

  • Does it fit the people I'm pitching first?
  • Does it make a strong impression in the first thirty seconds?
  • Does it represent the direction I want to be known for?
  • Is it technically ready, with artwork, credits and a clean version?
  • Would people who like it want to hear more of my music?

If a song gets a clear yes to all five, you have your lead.

After you choose

Once you have picked the song, build your list of contacts around it. Look for blogs, curators and radio hosts who already cover similar music, and write each of them a short, personal message. Choosing the first song is one of the few parts of promotion you fully control. Take the time to get it right, and every pitch that follows gets a little easier.
