Two Weeks Before Release: A Where-to-Submit Checklist

Two weeks before release is late, but it is not too late. Many independent artists only start thinking about submissions when the release date is close, and a fortnight is still enough time to run a focused, well-organised campaign. This checklist breaks the two weeks into manageable steps so you know where to submit, in what order and what to have ready.

Day 14 to Day 12: Get your materials in order

  • ☐ Final master uploaded to your distributor, with release date confirmed.
  • ☐ Private streaming link or download link that works without a login.
  • ☐ Clean version ready if the song has explicit lyrics.
  • ☐ Artwork in high resolution and a smaller version for messages.
  • ☐ Two or three press photos with photographer credits.
  • ☐ A short biography of four or five sentences.
  • ☐ A two-sentence description of the song: what it sounds like and what it is about.
  • ☐ Correct credits: writers, producers, featured artists.

Put everything in one shared folder with a single link. Every submission becomes faster once this is done.

Day 12 to Day 10: Build a focused list

With limited time, focus on fit rather than volume. Sort potential contacts into groups:

  • ☐ Music blogs that cover your genre and have written about artists at a similar stage.
  • ☐ Playlist curators whose playlists match your song's mood and style.
  • ☐ Radio hosts, especially specialist, community and online shows in your genre.
  • ☐ Local media: city blogs, student media, venue newsletters.
  • ☐ Creators who make content around your kind of music.

For each, note how they want to receive submissions. Respecting their process is the quickest way to be taken seriously.

Day 10 to Day 7: Send pre-release pitches

  • ☐ Prioritise blogs and radio hosts who like to hear music before release.
  • ☐ Consider offering one outlet a premiere, and only one.
  • ☐ Write each message personally: why them, what the song is, the release date, the link.
  • ☐ Keep messages short. The music should do most of the work.
  • ☐ Record every pitch in a simple sheet: name, date sent, reply.

Day 7 to Day 3: Fill the gaps

  • ☐ Pitch local media with a local angle.
  • ☐ Reach out to creators with a short clip they could use.
  • ☐ Prepare social posts and short videos for release week.
  • ☐ Update your streaming profiles, website and link-in-bio page with the new release.

Day 3 to Release Day: Prepare the follow-ups

  • ☐ Draft polite follow-ups for pre-release pitches with no reply.
  • ☐ Line up the curators who prefer released music; you'll pitch them once the song is live.
  • ☐ Plan who you'll thank and how you'll share any early coverage.

Release Day and after

  • ☐ Send follow-ups in the original threads with the live link.
  • ☐ Submit to playlist curators and shows that only take released music.
  • ☐ Share every piece of coverage and thank the people behind it.
  • ☐ Keep pitching for the next few weeks. A release doesn't stop being new after the weekend.

What to skip when time is short

With only two weeks, some things are better left out:

  • Long, mass-sent pitch emails to large, untargeted lists.
  • Any service that promises guaranteed streams or placements.
  • Contacts who clearly cover different genres, however big they are.

Spending your limited time on well-matched people will do more for the release.

Finding the right places quickly

The slowest part of a short campaign is usually research: working out which blogs, curators and radio hosts are worth contacting. Two weeks isn't ideal, but with clear materials, a focused list and a steady routine, it is enough to give a release a proper start. Next time, begin six weeks out and use the same checklist with more breathing room.
