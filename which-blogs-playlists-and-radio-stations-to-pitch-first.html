Which Blogs, Playlists and Radio Stations Should I Pitch First? A Seven-Question Filter

Ask how to find music blogs, playlists and radio stations for your genre and you usually get a long list of names. A list is a starting pool, not a plan. What decides whether a release gets covered is which few targets you pitch first, and that is a filtering problem. This guide, refreshed and expanded in November 2026, gives you seven yes-or-no questions, a simple scoring sheet, and the published rules from Spotify, KEXP and BBC Introducing that should shape the order you work in.

The short answer

Pitch first to the people who cover your genre, are active now, accept music the way you are sending it, support artists at your stage, and have a named person you can write to. Then give them a reason to care this week, and make sure you can explain the fit in one line. Everyone who passes all seven goes in your first wave; everyone else waits.

The seven questions

1. Do they cover my genre, not just a related one?

Look at the last ten posts, episodes or playlist additions. Your song should sound at home among them. "Indie" covers a lot of ground; a writer who loves bedroom pop may never touch post-punk. KEXP's John Richards suggests naming a few similar-sounding independent artists as "signposts" when you pitch radio, and those same names make a good test here: if a target already covers your signposts, the fit is real.

2. Are they active right now?

Recent posts, recent playlist updates and recent shows tell you someone is still there. A blog whose last review is from last year is not a first-wave target, however good its archive looks.

3. Do they support artists at my stage?

Some outlets only feature established names. Others exist to find new ones. KEXP's guide points artists toward non-commercial stations, student-run and low-power stations, and online stations as the best bets for airplay, and suggests local, new-music and specialty shows at commercial stations. The same logic holds for blogs and playlists: favour the ones that regularly feature artists you have not heard of.

4. Do they take music the way I am sending it?

Form, email, platform or not at all. Use the stated route. Some routes have hard rules that decide your timing:

  • Spotify's editorial playlists are pitched only through Spotify for Artists, for an unreleased song, one song at a time. Spotify asks for at least 7 days before release, and a song pitched that early also goes to followers' Release Radar. Once the song is live, it can no longer be pitched.
  • BBC Introducing, for UK-based artists, only considers tracks uploaded to its own Uploader, which sends them first to your nearest local Introducing show. Uploads are limited to two tracks in 30 days, and the BBC warns it can take up to six months to be heard.
  • Blogs and independent curators each set their own process. Read it, follow it, and never guess at private contact details.

5. Is there a clear person to write to?

A named writer, host, music director or curator is better than a generic inbox. KEXP notes that most stations have a music director, who at many stations decides rotation, and that specialty show hosts should get your music too.

6. Is there a reason to care now?

A release date, a local show, a video, a story behind the song: something that makes this week the right week to listen. Without one, even a good fit can drift to "later".

7. Can I explain the fit in one line?

"You covered two of my signposts this month, and this single sits between them." If you cannot write that line, the target is probably not a match. If you can, it becomes the first sentence of your message.

A scoring sheet

Pitch sevens and sixes first. Fours and fives go into a second wave once you have learned something from the first. Anything lower waits for a release that fits it better.

Order of operations

Some deadlines fall before release day and some only start after it, so work in this order:

  1. Before release: the Spotify editorial pitch (7 or more days out), any BBC Introducing upload you are happy to have played straight away, and premiere or review pitches to your top-scoring blogs.
  2. Release week: radio music directors and specialty shows that scored highly, with stream and download links, as KEXP asks.
  3. After release: independent playlist curators and second-wave blogs, which can cover a song weeks after it comes out.
  4. Two weeks later: polite follow-ups. KEXP's guide says to wait at least two weeks before following up on radio submissions.

What to avoid

  • Pitching a hundred targets at once with the same message.
  • Using one pitch for blogs, radio and playlists; each needs different details.
  • Ignoring replies, even short ones. "Not for us" tells you something about your next filter.

Bottom line

A short, filtered list beats a long, random one. Ask the seven questions, score your targets, send fewer and better pitches, and keep notes on every reply. For the blog side in more depth, see the guide to pitching music blogs from Virtual Publicist.
